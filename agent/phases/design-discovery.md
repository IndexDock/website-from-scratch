# Design discovery

## Goal

Translate business fit and visual preferences into a concrete design direction without copying references.

## Actions

Apply `agent/contracts/assets.md`, `agent/contracts/content-integrity.md`, `agent/contracts/icons.md`, `agent/contracts/typography.md`, `agent/contracts/state.md`, `agent/contracts/orchestration.md`, and `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md`.

The design interview is a procedure rather than a conversation to improvise, and its four steps run in the order given. The order is load-bearing. A translation written before the interview is a guess wearing the clothes of a decision, and an interview that opens before the references are on the table is a conversation about adjectives, which is the thing the references exist to replace. Each step ends with something written down, because a step whose result lives only in the conversation is a step the next session cannot tell was ever taken.

1. **Ask for reference screenshots, before any design question.** Explain what they buy the learner instead of simply demanding them. Words for visual qualities are unreliable: "clean", "modern", "professional" and "warm" mean a different thing to every person who says them, so a description on its own leaves the direction to be guessed at, and a guess costs rounds of correction later. A picture of a site the learner already likes removes that ambiguity in one step, and it is most of the difference between concepts that land close on the first attempt and concepts that need three passes to converge. Recommend them strongly and say so plainly. They are not compulsory — the decline path below is a real path, not a punishment — but the honest position is that the result is better with them.

   Make the request once and in full rather than in rounds, for the count and the reason `agent/contracts/assets.md` gives. Ask them to say in a line what caught their eye in each one where they can, because a screenshot with a sentence beside it is worth several without one.

   Say how to capture a screenshot, because many learners have never deliberately made one and will otherwise photograph the screen with a phone. On Windows, hold the Windows key, Shift and S together, then drag a box around the part of the page they mean; the capture goes to the clipboard and the notification that appears offers to save it as a file. Pressing Print Screen instead captures the whole screen. On macOS, hold Shift, Command and 4 together and drag the same kind of box, or Shift, Command and 3 for the whole screen; either way the image is saved to the desktop as a PNG file. These are operating-system instructions rather than agent instructions, which is why they belong in the workflow and not in a vendor entry point. Either a whole page or the one part that produced the reaction is fine.

   How the files reach the repository, where they are stored, and what happens to them at publication are settled in the "Reference screenshots" section of `agent/contracts/assets.md`. Follow it there rather than restating it here: it carries the upload route, the single narrow fallback that lets a screenshot be attached in the conversation instead, what that fallback costs, and the honest caveat about repository history.

   This request is the learner's `share-references` course checkpoint, titled "Share websites you like", which sits between `review-brief` and `choose-design`. Set `workflow.courseCheckpointId` to `share-references` when the request goes out, because that is the moment the learner has something of their own to do. It stays there until concepts are ready to show, which is when design review records `choose-design`.

2. **Interview the learner across the nine topics.** With references in hand, ask concretely — point at one screenshot and ask what they liked in that one — rather than asking abstract preference questions the references were meant to make unnecessary. The nine topics below are the coverage floor, and the interview cannot close until each one has a recorded answer:

   - **Lettering** — the shapes of the letters, and how large, heavy, and spaced out they are.
   - **Colour** — which colours belong to this business, which are welcome, and how much colour the page should carry.
   - **Spacing and density** — whether the page should feel roomy and unhurried or packed and busy.
   - **Layout and composition** — how the page is arranged: columns, asymmetry, wide bands, a grid the eye can feel.
   - **Imagery** — photographs, illustration, abstract shapes, or none at all, and what those pictures would be of.
   - **Shapes and edges** — rounded or square corners, visible borders and rules, hard or soft transitions.
   - **Animation** — whether anything should move, how much, and where.
   - **Overall mood** — the impression a visitor should be left with in the first few seconds.
   - **What must never appear** — anything the learner does not want on their site under any circumstances.

   Ask in the learner's own words rather than in craft vocabulary: "the lettering" rather than "typography", "how busy or roomy it feels" rather than "density". They are not designers, and a question they have to decode is a question that produces a shrug instead of an answer.

   Give "what must never appear" the same weight as the other eight and ask it directly, not as an afterthought once the useful questions are done. People articulate dislikes far more precisely than likes, and a dislike is immediately actionable where a like often is not: "no stock photographs of people shaking hands" and "nothing that flashes or slides in" each rule out a whole branch of the design space and can be tested against a finished concept, while "nice typography" can be neither acted on nor checked.

   Tag every observation the learner gives with one of two tiers, and write the tag down beside the observation rather than holding it in your head. **Match closely** means they want that element in their own site much as it stands. **Just the feel** means they like the impression it leaves but not its specifics. Both are legitimate answers and neither is the better one. The tag travels with the observation into the saved file, because it is what tells the design worker whether a reference is a target or an atmosphere: a worker reading "likes the big serif headings in the second screenshot" with no tier attached cannot tell whether reproducing that structure is the brief or a misreading of it.

   Where the learner has no opinion, record that as the answer. "No opinion, agent's choice" is a positive entry; a blank is not an entry at all. This is load-bearing rather than tidiness. A blank cannot be told apart from a topic that was never raised, so nobody reading the file afterwards — the next session, an auditor, or the learner — can tell whether the silence was the learner's or the agent's. And the topic where the learner offered no opinion is exactly the topic where `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md` decides instead, so recording the no-opinion is what makes it auditable afterwards which choices were the learner's and which were the rubric's. Never press for an opinion the learner does not have: ask once, accept the answer, write it down.

3. **Play back what you understood, and let the learner close the interview.** Write a short summary of what you heard, topic by topic, including the topics where they had no opinion and the tier recorded against each observation, and put it in front of them to confirm or extend. The learner ends the interview, not the agent: while they are still adding to the summary, the interview is still running.

   This play-back is also the "anything I missed?" step, and it is deliberately one moment rather than two. Asking "is there anything else?" in the abstract reliably produces "no", because nobody can audit a conversation from memory; asking someone to check a written list produces the correction that the open question never would. Append whatever the play-back adds to the record before moving on, in the same form as the rest, tier included.

4. **Translate the recorded answers into craft decisions.** Only now, with the interview closed, turn what the learner said into typography, composition, hierarchy, colour, geometry, density, imagery, rhythm, and motion. This is the agent's work rather than the learner's: they described impressions and reactions, and deciding which type pairing, scale, palette, and layout concept produce those impressions for this business is a craft judgement that `agent/contracts/orchestration.md` keeps off their desk. Where the record says no opinion, `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md` decides and its standing conventions govern. Save a bounded design packet containing only approved facts, content, assets, direction, and acceptance criteria.

A learner may decline to supply screenshots. That is a legitimate choice, not a failure of the phase. Record the decline in `project/DECISIONS.md` so a later session does not reopen a settled question, then cover the same nine topics by description. The principle is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to breach without noticing: the two paths differ in what prompts the conversation and never in what the conversation covers. Declining must not quietly downgrade the learner to a thinner process. Without references, lean harder on comparison and on the negative topic — offer two described treatments and ask which sounds closer, and spend longer on what must never appear, because a learner working without pictures usually finds it easier to say what is wrong than to say what is right. Declining costs the direction some precision, and it costs nothing else.

One floor holds regardless of tier, and it holds under **match closely** exactly as it holds under **just the feel**: never reproduce a reference's logo, its marks, its photography, its illustration, or its copy. What a reference legitimately supplies is structure, proportion, palette direction, mood, and rhythm — the qualities anybody may learn from — and never the assets that identify the business it belongs to. The signature element required by `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md` must be this business's own, arrived at for this business. Every reference is treated alike, whether it belongs to a direct competitor, a business in an unrelated trade, or a famous brand the learner simply admires. The rule is about someone else's property rather than about rivalry, and a special case for competitors would carry the false implication that everybody else is fair to copy.

`project/DESIGN-DIRECTION.md` holds two labelled sections, governed differently. **What the learner told us** is append-only and is never rewritten; it holds the nine topics, the answer recorded against each one including the no-opinions, and the tier on every observation. **Direction** is rewritable and holds the craft translation the design worker builds from: the palette direction, the type pairing and scale, the layout concept, the hero constraints, what the signature element should express, and the rest of the acceptance criteria.

The Direction states the hero constraints and the reason for them in every packet, whether or not references were supplied and whether or not the learner had an opinion on layout. It is prose of a sentence or two rather than a set of fields or a list of permitted compositions, because fields invite filling in and a permitted list becomes a menu. It states constraints rather than one fixed composition, since each concept picks its own hero within them and records its own reason, and that is what keeps the required concepts distinct while a **match closely** reference stays binding. Where the record shows no references and no opinion on layout, the constraints will rule little out and the standing convention in `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md` carries the hero decision, exactly as step four already says for any topic the learner had no opinion on.

The design worker builds from the Direction section. The interview section is evidence for why the direction says what it says, never a build instruction. It records a learner's reaction to somebody else's website, which is not the same thing as a decision about this one, and a worker building straight from it would be building the reference rather than the direction — the exact failure the tiers and the intellectual-property floor above exist to prevent.

The split exists because the two halves have different lifetimes. The packet is rewritten whenever the direction changes — after a revision round, after two concepts are combined, after a late constraint appears — and the interview is the most expensive input in this workflow to reproduce, because reproducing it means asking a person to find, capture, and upload their references again and sit through the questions a second time. Everything else in the packet can be rebuilt from files that still exist. Holding the interview in its own append-only section is what makes the rest of the packet safe to rewrite.

When the learner later contradicts something they said, which most often happens after seeing concepts and an abstract preference meets a real page, append the new statement as a dated correction beneath the original entry rather than editing over it. Both then stand, so it stays visible that they changed their mind and when. Editing the original in place destroys the more useful record of the two: that a stated preference did not survive contact with a rendered page is itself a finding, and the next direction should be built knowing it.

Reuse the saved asset inventory; do not ask for an asset again when its supplied, missing, or intentionally unnecessary status is already known. If a still-unknown asset materially affects the direction, ask the learner to upload it through GitHub's **Add file → Upload files**, with no folder or branch decision on their part, then fetch and place it yourself. The upload lands on `main`, so merge `origin/main` into the session branch first, as `agent/contracts/git-delivery.md` requires.

`project/DESIGN-DIRECTION.md` — the packet the design worker reads — may state the intended concept count, but only by repeating the rule in `agent/phases/design-review.md` as it stands: three by default, fewer only where a specific constraint narrows the space. Never soften it into a range. A packet that says "two to three concepts" has turned a default plus a condition into a free choice, and because the worker reads the packet rather than the phase module, the condition is then never tested against this project at all.

When fewer than three is justified, also append that reason to `project/DECISIONS.md`. Recording it in the packet alone is not enough: `agent/phases/design-review.md` requires the constraint to be named in `project/DECISIONS.md` before concepts are built, so a justification kept only in the packet leaves that precondition unmet. The two files hold it for different reasons. The packet briefs one worker for one phase and is rewritten whenever the direction changes; `project/DECISIONS.md` is the append-only record of what was decided and why, which is where a later session, an auditor, or the learner goes to find out why they were shown two concepts instead of three.

## Exit

Save and validate `project/DESIGN-DIRECTION.md` with enough detail for a clean-context design worker to create materially different concepts from the approved website plan. The packet must carry craft acceptance criteria a worker can be held to — the intended palette direction, the type pairing and scale, the layout concept, and what the signature element should express — so `agent/rubrics/design-craft.md` is testable rather than aspirational. Those criteria belong in the Direction section, which is the section the worker builds from.

The packet also names the icon weight and the arrow shape family from `agent/contracts/icons.md`, or says plainly that the direction uses no icons. Both follow from the type, so they belong here with it rather than being settled by whoever writes the first button. Do not ask the learner about either: this is a craft decision, and `agent/contracts/orchestration.md` keeps technical judgement off their desk. The type decision also sets the leading: `agent/contracts/typography.md` fixes three `line-height` tokens, tightens display headings to their own value, and asks that the headline be shaped, so the packet's type direction should say what the display leading and the headline shape are aiming for.

The interview section must also be complete before the phase exits: all nine topics answered, the no-opinions recorded as answers rather than left blank, every observation tagged with its tier, and the play-back confirmed by the learner. An interview that is missing a topic is not finished early, it is unfinished, and the gap surfaces as an invented assumption in a concept nobody can trace back.
